# [DOA Estimation] Master's Thesis from Tel Aviv University: DOA with Dynamic Array Configuration

We investigated two different scenarios regarding the direction of arrival (DOA). The first scenario involves a sensor moving slowly relative to the sampling rate, where a polynomial-based algorithm was developed. The second scenario involves rapid changes in configuration, for which an iterative method was proposed. This method can be used to simultaneously locate the azimuth and elevation angles of multiple transmitters. The performance of the proposed method was evaluated through Monte Carlo simulations and compared with the Cramer-Rao lower bound. Since the DOA algorithm requires evaluating array configurations, we discussed array calibration methods that are relevant to this scenario.
We derived a closed-form solution for self-calibration of closely spaced sensors. For sensor spacing greater than half a wavelength, we proposed an integer search-based localization method. We also considered the case of dual-polarization dynamic arrays. Furthermore, we derived an unscented Kalman filter-based direct localization method based on estimating the position of the radiation source.

Introduction
The problem of radiating source localization has attracted widespread attention in various disciplines such as signal processing, communication, automotive technology, and underwater acoustics. The most common method for locating radio signal transmitters is based on measuring specified parameters, such as the arrival angle (AOA) or time of arrival (TOA), which are estimated from received signals [1,2]. These parameters are then used to estimate the transmitter's position based on geometric considerations [1,2]. For example, each sensor provides AOA measurements, which produce straight line positions (LOP) passing through the sensor and transmitter locations when noise is ignored. When these LOPs intersect at the transmitter's location, as shown in Figure 1.1a. Similarly, when TOA measurements are taken, each measurement produces circular LOPs centered around the sensor and passing through the transmitter's location. The transmitter's position is determined by the intersection points of these circular LOPs, as shown in Figure 1.1b. When measurements have noise, the LOPs may not intersect at the same location, necessitating further position estimation.
Masako Kishida et al.'s work [21] introduced a method for estimating signal direction of arrival (DOA) by moving sensors. The key idea is to move the sensor along a precisely circular path to make phase modulation in the signal dependent on DOA. In another study, Zhan Shi et al. [22] proposed a method for creating pseudo-linear arrays by moving sensors linearly. The sensors are constrained to move in a specific precise manner and their movements are used for DOA estimation. In our work, we address the general case where no assumptions about the configuration of the sensors during measurement are made. We assume that the sensors move randomly and their observations are used for DOA estimation. So far, without reaching direction estimation or positioning problems based on general dynamically changing configurations, this challenge has been resolved. The purpose of this research is to develop methods for dynamically changing configurations of DOA and positioning.
When considering multipath channels, estimating these parameters becomes a major challenge and often involves channel model approximations. Bialer et al. [3] assumed that multipath arrivals are sufficiently dense and can be modeled as a Gaussian process, and constructed a maximum likelihood (ML) estimator for TOA when the assumptions hold, which achieves accurate ML performance. A more general approach to estimate TOA is using known statistical models instead of detailed multipath construction [4,5].
From an estimation perspective, since the first step parameter measurement is performed separately at each base station without using the constraint that all measurements must correspond to the same transmitter, they are suboptimal. Moreover, when multiple transmitters are considered, there is the issue of relating measured parameters to their associated sources.
Direction Estimation Technology can be divided into two main categories: spectral-based methods and parameterized methods. In the spectral-based approach, a class of spectrum functions is formed to estimate DOA, with the peak value corresponding to the function being declared as the estimated DOA. Generally, this method forms a two-dimensional spectrum. In the parameterized approach, the signal waveform is modeled as a random process, and DOA estimation is obtained by minimizing some statistical functions parameterized by DOA. The estimates from the parameterized method are more accurate. However, compared to spectral-based techniques, parameterized methods require significantly higher computational demands. Generally, when the dimensionality increases with the number of transmitters, multi-dimensional search is required in the parameterized approach. The use of parameterized algorithms for direction estimation problems involving coherent sources is preferred. Spectral-based techniques also fall into two different subcategories: beamforming techniques and subspace-based techniques. In beamforming techniques, the main idea is to steer the array and measure the output power. The point at which the output power peaks is recorded as the estimated DOA. Traditional beamformers, such as the Bartlett beamformer, are based on general extensions of Fourier's spectral analysis. Capon further developed the beamformer to improve its resolution for two closely spaced sources. In subspace-based techniques, the feature structure of the observation data covariance matrix is directly used to find the estimated DOA. The first subspace-based algorithm is called MUSIC (Multiple Signal Classification), introduced independently by Schmidt [8,9] and Bienvenu and Kopp [10,11]. For equally spaced linear arrays, there are effective algorithms for direction estimation available, namely root-MUSIC and ESPRIT (estimating signal parameters via rotation invariance) algorithms [12-14]. Compared to beamforming techniques, subspace-based algorithms have increased resolution due to the fact that no data windowing is needed. Spectral-based methods are computationally less demanding than parameterized methods. However, especially for scenarios involving highly correlated signals, the performance of spectral-based methods may not be sufficient. Parameterized methods produce more precise solutions. Unfortunately, these methods require multi-dimensional searches to find the estimated values, which is computationally complex. The most famous and commonly used parameterized algorithm is maximum likelihood (ML) technique [15]. Another important classification of DOA estimation methods can be completed based on the array geometry required by the technology used. Arrays with special sensor arrangements, particularly uniform linear arrays (Uniform Linear Array, or ESPRIT), offer opportunities for using high resolution, low computational requirements, and simpler subspace-based techniques like root-MUSIC [12], RARE [16], IQML [17], conventional and multiple invariant ESPRIT [13]. All these algorithms require uniform linear arrays and/or arrays with translation invariance. Another central issue is the estimation of DOA for completely correlated signals. This situation is known as coherent signal cases, which occur in mirror multipath propagation and is therefore of great practical significance. Evans et al. and Shan et al. [18,19] proposed a preprocessing technique called spatial smoothing for handling coherent signals, which allows for low complexity DOA methods to be used with coherent signals and was further developed by Pillai [20].
